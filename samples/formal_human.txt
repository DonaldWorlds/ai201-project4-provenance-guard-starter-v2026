The doctrine's persistence is attributable less to its coherence than to the institutional convenience it affords. Courts invoking it rarely interrogate its premises; they inherit it, apply it, and pass it downward largely unexamined. Professor Whitford's objection — that the rule conflates evidentiary sufficiency with substantive entitlement — has never been satisfactorily answered, though it has been cited approvingly in at least three subsequent opinions. One suspects the silence is deliberate. To concede the conflation would require reopening a line of authority stretching back four decades, and the appellate judiciary has shown little appetite for that particular excavation. The result is a doctrine that functions, in practice, as a presumption masquerading as a rule.
